# Evidence Card

## Source

Citation: Ophir, E., Nass, C., & Wagner, A. D. (2009). Cognitive Control in Media Multitaskers. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 106(37), 15583-15587.

Link: https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.0903620106

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106

## Source Type

- primary study
- cognitive psychology

## Research Context

The paper compares heavy and light media multitaskers on cognitive control tasks.

## Main Finding

Heavy media multitaskers showed poorer performance on measures involving interference from irrelevant stimuli and memory representations, and performed worse on a task-switching measure.

## Limitations

The study is correlational. It does not prove that media multitasking caused the deficits, nor does it prove that a specific browsing session is harmful. Later literature emphasizes mixed evidence and causality caveats.

## Evidence Grade

moderate for association; weak for causal claims.

## Relevance To DaD

Media-heavy, multi-stream browsing can be a plausible risk context, especially when combined with tab switching, recommender clicks, and origin decay. It should not become a standalone diagnosis or moral judgment.

## Product Implication

DaD can keep media/recommender/feed pressure as a scoring modifier. It should require converging evidence before escalating to prompt or block.

## Claim Boundary

What DaD may safely say:

Heavy media multitasking is associated with differences in attention and cognitive control, so DaD treats media-multitasking contexts as possible drift pressure.

What DaD must not claim:

DaD must not claim media multitasking damages the brain, causes attention disorders, or proves the user is impaired.

## Notes

Use as a cautionary signal source, not as a hard-block justification.
