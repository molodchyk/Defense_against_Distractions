# Evidence Card

## Source

Citation: Himmler, O., Jaeckle, R., & Weinschenk, P. (2019). Soft Commitments, Reminders, and Academic Performance. American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11(2), 114-142.

Link: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.20170288

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170288

## Source Type

- primary study
- field experiment
- education
- behavioral economics

## Research Context

Students were offered a nonbinding agreement to stay on track for graduation. The study compared the soft commitment with reminder-only treatment.

## Main Finding

Soft commitment improved exam progression, while pure reminders did not change behavior. Procrastinators benefited most from the soft commitment.

## Empirical Detail

- Intervention: a nonbinding agreement to stay on track for graduation.
- Outcomes: exam sign-up, exam participation, and passing.
- Comparison: pure reminder treatment versus soft commitment.
- Direction of effect: offered soft commitment increased exam progression; reminders alone did not.
- Heterogeneity: procrastinators benefited most.

## Non-Obvious Mechanism

The effect was not just salience. A self-endorsed commitment can change later behavior in ways that reminder pings do not.

## Limitations

Education and browsing differ. The source does not tell DaD exactly how to phrase prompts or when to show them.

## Evidence Grade

moderate to strong.

## Relevance To DaD

DaD prompts should create or reactivate a concrete commitment, not merely nag. A reason prompt, return plan, or queued review has more mechanism than a generic reminder.

## Design Consequence

For ambiguous cases, DaD should prefer soft commitment actions like "return at break", "review this after lock", or "keep this plan for this session" over bare reminders.

## What Changes

This elevates soft commitment from secondary UX to a real defense layer. It also weakens the assumption that reminder notifications are enough.

## Notes

Relevant for reason prompts, soft locks, and post-lock review copy.
