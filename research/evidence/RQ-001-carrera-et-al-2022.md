# Evidence Card

## Source

Citation: Carrera, M., Royer, H., Stehr, M., Sydnor, J., & Taubinsky, D. (2022). Who Chooses Commitment? Evidence and Welfare Implications. The Review of Economic Studies, 89(3), 1205-1244.

Link: https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/89/3/1205/6367787

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdab056

## Source Type

- primary study
- field experiment
- structural behavioral economics

## Research Context

The authors studied gym-attendance commitment contracts and separately elicited actual and perceived time inconsistency.

## Main Finding

Commitment demand was high and behavior changed, but demand was poorly targeted. About half of those taking contracts for greater gym attendance also took contradictory contracts for lower attendance. Take-up had little association with measured actual or perceived time inconsistency, and structural estimates implied lower consumer surplus than linear subsidies achieving the same average behavior change.

## Empirical Detail

- Sample: 1,248 gym members.
- Intervention: commitment contracts restricting or penalizing future attendance choices without financial upside.
- Key result: approximately half of "more attendance" commitment takers also accepted "less attendance" contracts.
- Mechanism test: a separate elicitation task measured actual and perceived time inconsistency.
- Welfare result: structural model estimates implied that commitment contracts lowered consumer surplus relative to behaviorally equivalent linear exercise subsidies.

## Non-Obvious Mechanism

Demand for commitment is not reliable evidence of accurate self-knowledge. Commitment can be selected because of noise, misunderstanding, unstable valuation, or incorrect beliefs about one's own time inconsistency.

## Limitations

Gym attendance differs from browsing. Consumer-surplus estimates rely on structural assumptions. Still, the targeting failure is directly relevant to any product that interprets a user's request for strictness as proof that strictness is well calibrated.

## Evidence Grade

strong.

## Relevance To DaD

DaD users can sincerely request strong locks that are not actually good for them later. A strict configuration choice is a signal of desired help, not a diagnosis of the correct lock.

## Design Consequence

DaD should add lock calibration infrastructure: outcome history, post-lock review, delayed relaxation, and warnings when a user repeatedly contests the same lock.

## What Changes

This is the strongest source against naive "more blocking equals better defense." DaD should optimize for calibrated protection, not just fewer bypasses or higher block counts.

## Notes

This source should guide the first implementation pass after the research: local lock outcome categories.
