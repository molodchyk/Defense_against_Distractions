# Evidence Card

## Source

Citation: Roffarello, A. M., & De Russis, L. (2023). Achieving Digital Wellbeing Through Digital Self-Control Tools: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction, 30(4), 1-66.

Link: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3571810

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3571810

## Source Type

- systematic review
- meta-analysis
- HCI

## Research Context

The paper reviews and meta-analyzes research on digital self-control tools, including motivations, strategies, design choices, and evaluation challenges.

## Main Finding

Digital self-control tools are promising but the evidence base has important gaps. The research literature needs better long-term studies, standardized measures, and more attention to ethics and business-model context.

## Empirical Detail

- Source type: systematic review and meta-analysis of digital self-control tools.
- Scope: tools using interventions such as timers, lockout mechanisms, self-monitoring, and other technology-use regulation strategies.
- Reported focus: motivations, strategies, design choices, evaluation challenges, and overall effect on reducing unwanted technology use.
- Field-wide gaps: long-term studies, standardized measures, and ethical treatment of business-model incentives.

## Non-Obvious Mechanism

The research gap itself is product-relevant. DaD cannot outsource validation to the existence of digital wellbeing literature; it needs local outcome metrics because the field lacks enough long-term, standardized evidence.

## Limitations

The paper is about digital self-control broadly. It does not give a direct answer for DaD's stricter-only lock editing rule.

## Evidence Grade

strong for the state of evidence in digital self-control tools; moderate for direct locked-schedule policy.

## Relevance To DaD

This source is a caution against overclaiming. DaD can use digital self-control patterns, but it should locally validate whether locked schedules help the target user and should avoid claiming that the whole system is already scientifically proven.

## Design Consequence

DaD should build local validation loops:

- how often relaxation is attempted during locked schedules;
- whether blocked relaxation attempts are followed by successful return to plan;
- whether the user later regrets a lock;
- whether lock duration or scope needs adjustment;
- whether emergency escape was needed.

These should stay local-first and aggregate by default.

## What Changes

This turns lock calibration into an evidence problem inside DaD. The extension should measure local outcomes of locks before making broad product claims or defaulting users into stronger protection.

## Notes

This source supports humility in store listing and public explanation.
