# Evidence Card

## Source

Citation: Lyngs, U., Lukoff, K., Slovak, P., Binns, R., Slack, A., Inzlicht, M., Van Kleek, M., & Shadbolt, N. (2019). Self-Control in Cyberspace: Applying Dual Systems Theory to a Review of Digital Self-Control Tools. Proceedings of CHI 2019.

Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00157

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300361

## Source Type

- HCI review
- digital self-control tools

## Research Context

The authors analyzed 367 apps and browser extensions from app stores and the Chrome Web Store, identifying common design features and intervention strategies. They organized these tools using a dual-systems self-regulation framework.

## Main Finding

Digital self-control tools commonly use mechanisms such as blocking, timers, limits, feedback, goals, and friction to help users regulate digital device use. The paper also argues that the design space remains underdeveloped and that tools should be evaluated through self-regulation mechanisms, not just feature lists.

## Limitations

The paper maps the design landscape; it does not prove that every tool or feature works. It also does not directly test DaD's locked-schedule policy.

## Evidence Grade

moderate.

## Relevance To DaD

DaD belongs to the digital self-control tool category but is more layered than a simple blocker. Locked schedules are a recognized style of digital self-control intervention, especially when combined with blocking and friction.

## Product Implication

DaD should keep its layered intervention design:

- block when the user precommitted;
- add friction when the situation is ambiguous;
- show diagnostics;
- avoid pretending that usage stats alone are enough;
- evaluate mechanisms: what problem does this lock solve?

## Claim Boundary

What DaD may safely say:

DaD uses established digital self-control patterns such as blocking, friction, scheduling, and local feedback.

What DaD must not claim:

DaD must not claim that its exact combination has already been validated by the HCI literature.

## Notes
