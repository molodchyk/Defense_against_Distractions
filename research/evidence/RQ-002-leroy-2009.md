# Evidence Card

## Source

Citation: Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181.

Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v109y2009i2p168-181.html

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002

## Source Type

- primary study
- experiments
- organizational psychology

## Research Context

The paper studies what happens when people switch between work tasks, especially when the previous task is unfinished. It introduces attention residue as a mechanism explaining why attention does not instantly transition from one task to another.

## Main Finding

Across experiments, people had difficulty fully moving attention away from an unfinished prior task, and performance on the subsequent task suffered. Finishing the prior task helped, but was not always enough; the conditions around the transition mattered.

## Limitations

The tasks were controlled work tasks, not browsing sessions. The source does not directly validate a browser extension score or DaD's exact Return/Isolate actions.

## Evidence Grade

moderate to strong for attention residue; moderate for DaD application.

## Relevance To DaD

DaD's intent-drift model assumes that switching away from an originating task can leave a residual unresolved goal. This source supports the idea that abrupt or unfinished transitions can impose cognitive cost.

## Product Implication

Return is scientifically plausible: if the current chain drifted from the original objective, returning to the last coherent page can help close or resume the prior trajectory.

Isolate is also plausible, but more indirectly: if the current page is genuinely intentional, isolating it into a new chain can reduce ambiguity by declaring a new task boundary rather than keeping it attached to the old origin.
