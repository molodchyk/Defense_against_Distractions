# Evidence Card

## Source

Citation: Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When Choice Is Demotivating: Can One Desire Too Much of a Good Thing? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(6), 995-1006.

Link: https://faculty.washington.edu/jdb/345/345%20Articles/Iyengar%20%26%20Lepper%20%282000%29.pdf

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995

## Source Type

- primary experiments
- choice and motivation

## Research Context

The paper tested whether larger choice sets improve or reduce motivation and satisfaction across field and laboratory contexts.

## Main Finding

Limited arrays sometimes produced more commitment and satisfaction than extensive arrays. The classic conditions compared 6 options with 24 or 30 options.

## Empirical Detail

- Studies: three experiments.
- Domains: gourmet jams, chocolates, optional class essays.
- Finding: people were more likely to purchase or undertake assignments with limited choice sets.
- Additional finding: participants reported greater satisfaction or produced better essays when options were limited rather than expanded.

## Non-Obvious Mechanism

More options can create decision burden and reduce commitment, even though people often prefer the idea of more choice.

## Limitations

Choice overload is context-dependent and not universally replicated as a main effect. This card must be paired with Scheibehenne et al. 2010.

## Evidence Grade

moderate as a design warning; not sufficient alone.

## Relevance To DaD

Enforcement moments are already high-friction. A large menu of escape, edit, inspect, delay, return, isolate, and configure options may feel like control while degrading action.

## Design Consequence

Each intervention surface should show a small primary action set, with advanced options one layer deeper.

## What Changes

DaD should preserve real bounded choice but avoid turning every lock into a settings dashboard.

## Notes

Use this source cautiously; the stronger conclusion is refined by the meta-analysis card.
